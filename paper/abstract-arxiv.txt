Every practical agent-memory system curates, and every curator decides on
some signal: a classifier's verdict, a judge's rating, a measured outcome.
The memory-security literature defends the store at write time (does this
text look malicious?) and at retrieval (should this be used?). Recent
surveys name a third possibility but do not measure it: an adversary
attacking the deciding signal itself. We make that threat quantitative. In a
curation-targeted attack the adversary injects no poison and instead
corrupts settlement, so the defence starves the memory it protects -
denial of memory. We measure six curation mechanisms against it at five
attack budgets over 30 seeds.

The separation is sharp and so is its boundary. At one corrupted
measurement per cycle, every strike counter and quarantine policy we test
loses most or all benign capability - an evict-on-negative counter loses
all of it - while a bounded-credit energy ledger loses none. A
Thompson-sampling bandit keeps capability too, but only by never removing
anything: retention is not defence. The ledger's own boundary lies between
two and four lies per cycle and we publish it, not just the win. An adversary buying persistence rather than destruction removes the
ledger's guarantee silently, while benign capability never moves.

We then run the same attack on real software-engineering tasks.
Capability does not separate there: on that benchmark memory buys none to
begin with. What transfers is the mechanism in its proper currency: at 17%
of settlements corrupted, a counter's deletion of benign memory roughly
triples while the ledger's barely moves. A second repository does not
reproduce that; the ordering alone holds in 5 of 6 worlds. We give a regime map saying which curator
to use when, including where a one-line heuristic wins, and
release a stdlib-only harness with pre-registered hypotheses and
manifest-checked per-seed data.
