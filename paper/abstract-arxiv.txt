Every practical agent-memory system curates, and every curator decides on
some signal: a classifier's verdict, a judge's rating, a measured outcome.
The memory-security literature defends the store at write time and at
retrieval, and both read content. Recent surveys name a third possibility
but do not measure it: an adversary attacking the deciding signal itself.
We make that threat quantitative. In a curation-targeted attack the
adversary injects no poison and instead corrupts settlement, so the defence
starves the memory it protects - denial of memory. We measure six curation
mechanisms at five attack budgets over 30 seeds.

At one corrupted measurement per cycle, the strike counters and quarantine
policies in our grid keep at most about half their benign capability, while
a bounded-credit energy ledger keeps all of it. A Thompson-sampling bandit
keeps capability too, but only by never removing anything: retention is not
defence. We publish the ledger's own boundary, between two and four lies per
cycle, not just the win.

That counter result covers four settings, so we swept the family's whole
dial, and it refuted our pre-registered prediction: a forgiveness counter
tuned to k=5 reaches the ledger's corner. It gets there by barely evicting,
and pays on an axis the corner does not price - 16% less measured outcome
from a store four times larger (p=0.0003). The lifetime-strike shape has no
working setting at any k we tested.

We then run the same attack on real software-engineering tasks. Capability
does not separate: across 2,115 SWE-Bench-CL tasks no memory arm beat
carrying no memory at all, and what selection buys there is leanness. The
ordering alone transfers, in 5 of 6 worlds, and one repository does not
reproduce it. We give a regime map saying which curator to use when, and
release a stdlib-only harness with pre-registered hypotheses and
manifest-checked per-seed data.
