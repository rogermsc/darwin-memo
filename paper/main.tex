\begin{abstract}
Agent memory that only grows is eventually dominated by stale and
poisoned entries, so every practical system curates. Every curator
decides on some signal---a classifier's verdict, a judge's rating, a
measured outcome---and the memory-security literature defends that store
at \emph{write} (does this text look malicious?) and at \emph{retrieval}
(should this be injected?). Recent work names a third possibility
without measuring it: an adversary who attacks the deciding signal
itself. We make that threat quantitative. In a
\emph{curation-targeted attack} the adversary injects no poison at all
and instead corrupts settlement so that the defence starves the
defender's own memory---denial of memory---and we measure six curation
mechanisms against it at five attack budgets over 30 seeds.
The separation is sharp and so is its boundary. At one corrupted
measurement per cycle, every strike counter and quarantine policy we
test loses most or all benign capability---an evict-on-negative
counter loses all of it---while a bounded-credit energy ledger loses
none and retains $99.4\%$ of its unattacked outcome. A
Thompson-sampling bandit also keeps capability, but only by never
removing anything: it hoards dead weight and its poison-kill rate falls
to $0.07$ by four lies per cycle. Retention is not defence. The ledger's
own failure boundary lies between two and four lies per cycle and we
publish it rather than stopping at the win.
A second and weaker adversary closes a second named-but-unmeasured gap:
one who never writes, never settles, and only asks questions still drives
a usage-based retention score, and thereby owns roughly the last $13\%$
of the store's lifetime---stable across two corpora and four upkeep
settings, and absent in all $32$ cells under the flat upkeep we ship. A
one-denominator counterfactual isolates the cause, and it is not that the
signal is \emph{usage} but that it is \emph{relative}: one entry's
standing is a function of every other entry's traffic, so the adversary
takes lifetime from the benign population instead of buying its own.
We then run the same attack on real software-engineering tasks, and
report which half of that result survives contact with them. Capability
does not separate there and no mechanism clears the poison, because on
that benchmark memory buys no measurable capability to begin with---an
attack on memory cannot cost more than the memory was worth. What does
transfer is the mechanism in its proper currency: at $17\%$ of
settlements corrupted, the counter's deletion of \emph{benign} memory
roughly triples while the ledger's barely moves, on every seed. That leg
is one repository at three seeds, too few to carry a $p$-value, and we
report it as effect size and unanimity rather than dressing it as
significance.
The ledger we defend with is \textsc{darwin-memo}, in which each entry
pays energy upkeep and earns only from a conserved resource the
environment already measures---no reward model, no judge, no labels.
We are equally precise about where it is unnecessary. Absent an
adversary a one-line heuristic ties it on outcomes, a confidence bandit
matches it under one-sided noise, and on real software-engineering tasks
it shows no advantage over a token-matched random control---across
$2{,}115$ evaluated SWE-Bench-CL tasks on four sequences, including a
matrix long enough that the mechanism verifiably removes half of what it
writes, no memory arm beat carrying no memory at all. What it does buy
there is leanness: the same capability from half the store. We give the
regime map that says which tool to use when, and report every one of
those losses. Every number comes from a stdlib-only harness with
pre-registered hypotheses, seed-level bootstrap intervals, exact paired
permutation tests, and committed per-seed data validated against its
manifest in CI.
\end{abstract}
