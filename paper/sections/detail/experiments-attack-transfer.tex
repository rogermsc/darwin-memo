% django's tripling, sympy's absence of it, and the sixth world that
% reversed the ordering. The body states the scope this leaves; these
% are the numbers it was read off.
\paragraph{What transfers is a mechanism on one repository, not a law.}
The attack's damage lands entirely on benign memory, and how much
depends on whether removal is recoverable (Table~\ref{tab:swebench-attack}).
On \texttt{django}, under the same six lies, the counter's benign burial
roughly triples while the ledger's barely moves, on every seed, with
non-overlapping ranges ($2.29$--$3.00$ against $1.08$--$1.43$). The
trigger lists show the adversary doing both halves of its job: on seed 0
it flipped two genuine damage signals positive so the guilty went
unpunished, and manufactured six against benign work, and the counter
obeyed on every count. That is the argument of \S\ref{sec:adversary}
measured on real software-engineering tasks: a counter spends a life it
cannot recover, so a lie costs it an entry permanently; a ledger spends
energy the entry earns back, so the same lie costs it almost nothing.

\emph{A second repository does not reproduce the magnitude, and we lead
with that.} On \texttt{sympy} the tripling is absent---the counter's
burial ratio is $0.96$, $0.93$ and $0.79$ against the ledger's $0.78$,
$0.76$ and $0.84$---and neither mechanism is meaningfully damaged. The
reason is visible in the unattacked column and is a scope condition we
had no way to see from one repository: on \texttt{django} the counter
buries $6$--$7$ benign entries with no adversary present, on
\texttt{sympy} $24$--$27$. A mechanism already destroying four times as
much benign memory at baseline has little headroom left for an adversary
to exploit. The tripling is therefore a property of a curator that is
otherwise behaving well, not of the attack.

\emph{The ordering does not survive the sixth world either.} Five of the
worlds gave a unanimous ordering: the counter's burial ratio above the
ledger's in every one, differences $+0.17$ to $+1.92$, exact paired
permutation $p = 0.0625$, the floor at five pairs. We ran the
pre-registered sixth to move that floor to $0.031$ and the sign reversed
instead ($0.79$ against $0.84$, a difference of $-0.05$), so the count is
$5$ of $6$ and the permutation stays at $p = 0.0625$---the reversal is
small enough that flipping it back would \emph{raise} the mean
difference, which is why the test cannot reach its new floor. Split by
repository the ordering is plainly \texttt{django}'s: $+1.17$ to $+1.92$
there against $+0.18$, $+0.17$ and $-0.05$ on \texttt{sympy}, mean
$+0.10$, $p = 0.50$. We read it as a single-repository effect that
\texttt{sympy} neither confirms nor contradicts, and report the
completed design as having returned a negative answer rather than a
weaker positive one.
