\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Every agent-memory defence is itself an attack surface, because every
defence decides on a signal an adversary can reach. We defined the
curation-targeted attack, in which no poison is injected and the
curator is instead fed corrupted evidence until it starves the memory it
was protecting, and measured six mechanisms under it. The mechanisms
separate on one property: whether a false signal costs a life or costs
energy that can be won back. Counters spend lives and are destroyed at
one lie per cycle; a bandit keeps its capability only by never removing
anything and stops defending; a bounded credit buffer with earn-back
survives, and then fails too, between two and four lies per cycle. We
publish that boundary because a defence whose limit is unstated is a
defence nobody can deploy safely.

The ledger we defend with, \textsc{darwin-memo}, needs no reward model,
no judge, and no labels: settlement is a conserved quantity the
environment already returns, four to six orders of magnitude cheaper
than asking a model. But we are precise about how far that carries.
Outside an adversarial regime a one-line heuristic ties it on outcomes
and, with a real model deciding adoption, revokes poison sooner; a
confidence bandit matches it under one-sided noise; and on real
software-engineering tasks it shows no advantage over a token-matched
random control on sequences shorter than its own starvation horizon.
The regime map is our second contribution precisely because those
losses are real, and a reader deciding what to deploy is better served
by knowing which axis they are on than by a claim of dominance our own
tables would refute.

What we would most like tested next is the threat model rather than the
mechanism. If curation-targeted attacks are as general as they appear,
every published memory defence---write-time filter, retrieval sanitizer,
LLM judge---owes an answer to the question of what happens when its
deciding signal is the thing under attack. All code, per-seed data, and
pre-registered protocols are released under a permissive license with
manifest-checked reproducibility.
