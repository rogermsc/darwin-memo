\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Memory poisoning, and where its defences sit.}
\citet{mpbench2026} systematize memory-poisoning attacks on LLM agents
and evaluate content-based detectors against them, reporting the split
that motivates this work: every off-the-shelf detector they test loses
between $18$ and $42$ points of true-positive rate moving from
strong-signal payloads to weak-signal ones---payloads written to read
like ordinary operational facts. The best of them, PromptArmor, falls
from $84.44\%$ to $42.50\%$; the weakest, DataFilter, from $28.86\%$ to
$10.74\%$. Their own reading is that the limitation is structural:
weak-signal payloads ``carry no syntactic anomaly'' and are
``semantically indistinguishable from legitimate content.''
\citet{memsecbench2026} track poisoning through a lifecycle, from
persistence to consequence to repair, and identify selective repair as
the open stage: removing poison without destroying benign memory
alongside it. We adopt their Write--Execute--Forget checkpoint structure
as a protocol (\S\ref{sec:wef}), reimplemented rather than ported, and
we make no comparison to their numbers.

What both share with the wider defence literature is \emph{where} the
defence sits: at write, screening text before it is stored, or at
retrieval, screening entries before they are injected. Both read
content, and content is exactly what a weak-signal payload is
constructed to survive. Our threat model is orthogonal to that axis
rather than a better position on it. We do not ask whether the defence
can recognize the payload; we ask what the defence does when the
evidence it decides on has itself been tampered with.

\paragraph{Attacking the curator: named, and left unmeasured.}
We are not the first to describe this threat, and we are explicit about
that because the distinction between naming it and measuring it is the
whole of our contribution here. \citet{lin2026selfevolving} catalogue
the attack surface of self-evolving agent systems and state the model
directly: an adversary who can ``shift embedding proximity or corrupt
the performance signal gains indirect control over the long-term
composition of the agent's cognitive state without ever writing to the
memory store directly,'' with the consequence that ``biased selection
can systematically retain harmful memories while discarding
safety-critical ones.'' Their case study CS-I6 names the same mechanism
as \emph{selective amplification}. \citet{lin2026memsurvey} organize
memory security around a lifecycle whose final phase is
\emph{Forget \& Rollback}, and place denial-style consequences---silent
eviction under retention pressure, denial of retrieval---in its
availability column.

Both also record that the threat is unmeasured. \citet{lin2026selfevolving}
write that ``direct attacks on memory consolidation policies remain
relatively underexplored''; \citet{lin2026memsurvey} report that
availability threats on agent memory are ``architecturally plausible but
empirically unstudied'' and that peer-reviewed availability-specific
attacks and defences are scarce enough that the area is a research
agenda rather than a subfield. That is the gap this paper closes. Our
contribution is not the observation that a curator can be attacked
through its evidence; it is the operationalization---a resource-bounded
adversary, six curation mechanisms, five attack budgets, thirty seeds,
per-seed intervals, exact paired permutation tests, and a published
failure boundary for the mechanism we defend with---together with a
real-task leg that reports which half of that result transfers and which
does not (\S\ref{sec:swebench-attack}).

\paragraph{Memory as a model, and the frozen executive.}
\citet{quek2026memo} argue for keeping the executive LLM frozen and
placing new knowledge in a dedicated memory model, with a
reflection-style QA encoding pipeline and a multi-stage query protocol.
We adopt their separation of executive from memory and their encoding
structure (\S\ref{sec:method}), but our live store is a structured set of
self-contained QA entries rather than a fine-tuned model, because
selection must operate on \emph{individual} entries. We close the loop to
the parametric form as a secondary result: distilling the survivors into
a small model (\S\ref{sec:experiments}) shows that selection survives the
round-trip into weights.

\paragraph{Environment-mediated selection.}
\citet{dodgson2026survival} introduce sustainable self-training in which
behaviors persist by environmental viability under a conserved resource
and bounded memory, rather than by an explicit reward, precisely to avoid
the reward-hacking and semantic-drift that an optimizable proxy invites.
We take this principle as our foundation and apply it at the granularity
of memory entries: energy is the survival currency, upkeep is the cost of
existing, and the only income is a conserved, measured resource delta.
Where their agents propose executable behaviors, our entries propose
answers from which the environment reads an action, a smaller attack
surface with the same selection geometry.

\paragraph{Hand-designed memory scores.}
\citet{park2023generative} score memories for retrieval by a weighted sum
of recency, importance (an LLM-rated poignancy), and query relevance.
This is the canonical hand-designed memory-management heuristic, and we
include it as an \emph{external baseline} (\S\ref{sec:experiments}): we
repurpose the recency$+$importance core of their score as a retention
rule and ask whether a hand-designed salience signal curates as well as a
conserved-resource ledger. (Because a deterministic, always-in-CI arm
cannot call an LLM, we proxy their LLM-rated importance with consultation
frequency and omit their query-conditioned relevance term, which is a
retrieval property, not a retention one; we state this deviation
explicitly.) MemGPT \citep{packer2023memgpt} manages a context hierarchy
by paging rather than by selection; Reflexion \citep{shinn2023reflexion}
and ExpeL \citep{zhao2024expel} accumulate verbal lessons across episodes;
Voyager \citep{wang2023voyager} grows a skill library. None of these
removes entries by a conserved-resource criterion.

\paragraph{Bandits over retrieval policies.}
\citet{xu2026ael} present AEL, which uses Thompson-sampling bandit
learning to \emph{select among retrieval policies} and LLM-driven
reflection to evolve behavior across long episode sequences. AEL is a
positive method for using memory well, not an argument against energy
ledgers; we are careful not to attribute such a claim to it. What it
motivates is a sharp control for \emph{our} mechanism: if a simple
confidence bandit over per-entry outcomes curates as well as a conserved
ledger, the ledger is unnecessary. We therefore reconstruct a per-entry
successive-elimination bandit with a Hoeffding radius
(\S\ref{sec:experiments}) and report exactly the regime---one-sided
measurement noise---where it matches survival, and the three things it
cannot do (starve dead weight, kill promptly, survive lies that pay the
guilty).

\paragraph{LLM-curated memory and its failure mode.}
\citet{zhang2026faulty} document that memory banks an LLM continuously
rewrites and consolidates degrade over time: useful memories become
faulty as the rewriting accumulates, because the model grades and
restates plausibility rather than verified consequence. Their finding
motivates a control we call \texttt{judge\_settled}, which is \emph{one
operationalization} of LLM-controlled memory updates---keep/cull verdicts
from a local model in place of measured settlement. We are explicit that
this is our construction, not a reproduction of their setup, and we report
a mixed result (\S\ref{sec:experiments}): the degradation appears for one
local judge and not another at our scale, so the broad claim that
conserved-resource settlement is categorically better is one our
experiments do \emph{not} make. What is unambiguous is cost: judged
settlement is $10^4$--$10^6\times$ slower than reading a scalar the
environment already returns.

\paragraph{Continual-learning evaluation for coding agents.}
SWE-bench \citep{jimenez2024swebench} evaluates issue resolution on real
GitHub repositories; \citet{joshi2025swebenchcl} organize its
human-verified subset into per-repository continual-learning sequences.
We adopt SWE-Bench-CL as the real-task testbed for whether a lesson store
under conserved-resource selection produces a learning curve across a
sequence (\S\ref{sec:experiments}); the resource is the change in
passing-test count returned by the official evaluation harness.
\citet{agentcl2026} raise the objection that bounds what any such leg can
show: benchmarks built from ``naive task streams'' do not guarantee that
earlier work is reusable later, so a memory arm and a no-memory arm can
be indistinguishable for reasons that have nothing to do with curation.
They propose controlled streams in which reuse is constructed. We report
our null with that caveat attached rather than as a property of the
mechanism (\S\ref{sec:limitations}).

\paragraph{What is stored, as opposed to what survives.}
An orthogonal axis to ours is representation. \citet{reasoningbank2026}
store distilled reasoning strategies rather than episodes, selected by an
agent judging its own successful and failed trajectories, and report
$54.0 \rightarrow 57.4$ resolve on SWE-Bench-Verified with
Gemini-2.5-pro. \citet{cl2memory2026} give the mechanism a name: memory
does not dissolve the stability--plasticity dilemma but relocates it to
retrieval, where abstract procedural memories transfer more reliably than
detailed trajectories. Every arm in this paper stores an episode and
varies only which episodes survive, so their axis and ours are
independent and untested against each other. We flag this as an open
comparison rather than a resolved one; the selection mechanism we study
is compatible with either representation, and their selection rule---an
LLM grading its own trajectories---is the one this work sets out to do
without.

\paragraph{Attacks that never write.}
The attack literature this defence answers to is older and broader than
the 2026 benchmarks. \citet{chen2024agentpoison} red-team agents by
poisoning memory and knowledge bases directly; \citet{zou2026poisononce}
inject through the environment a web agent reads;
\citet{srivastava2025memorygraft} achieve persistent compromise through
poisoned experience retrieval; and \citet{xu2026steering} target the
memory control flow rather than its contents. The case that matters most
for our threat model is \citet{dong2025minja}, which injects memory
through \emph{query-only} interaction: the attacker never writes, and
therefore never has to defeat a write-time filter or compromise the
settler. Our trust boundary assumes a settler that may be adversarial
(\S\ref{sec:adversary}); it does not cover an adversary who reaches memory
without settling at all, and outcome-grounded revocation has no claim
over an entry no consequence is ever attributed to.

\paragraph{Budgets, admission, and the round trip into weights.}
Two systems share our shape without sharing our signal.
\citet{admission2026} gate memory at write time on measured signals
rather than a judge, against MemGPT, MemoryBank, A-MEM
\citep{xu2025amem}, Mem0 \citep{mem0} and HippoRAG---the nearest
published mechanism to ours, and one evaluated with no adversary at all.
\citet{ember2026} retain evidence under a fixed budget, which is the
economic framing we use, with the difference that their budget is spent
on relevance while ours is \emph{earned} from outcomes: an entry that
looks useful keeps its place there, an entry that has been useful keeps
its place here. Downstream, \citet{tmem2026} absorb distilled experience
into fast LoRA weights online, and related work distils long-term memory
into parameters periodically. None of these applies a principled filter
to what gets distilled, which is the question our distillation arm
isolates (\S\ref{sec:experiments}): the same corpus, filtered three ways,
produces a model that reproduces the poison and a model that does not.

\paragraph{Learned credit assignment for memory operations.}
A parallel line keeps the memory operations and learns the credit.
\citet{memoryr2} optimize store/retrieve/update decisions with
group-relative policy optimization over trajectory-level reward;
\citet{treecredit2026} distribute credit across a multi-agent memory
system; \citet{attrimem2026} attribute process-level feedback to
individual memory contents. They share a stated bottleneck---outcome
signals are too coarse to say which memory content earned the result---and
answer it with learning. The ledger answers the same question with
arithmetic: credit is bounded by $\tanh$ and scoped to provenance, so
attribution is a property of the write path rather than an inference
about it. Neither approach is free; ours buys auditability and pays in
resolution.
